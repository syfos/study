# Kinship, Caste and Class — Quick Notes

*Timeline: c. 600 BCE – 600 CE*

## Language and Social Divide
- **Sanskrit** — language of the upper class / Brahmins / elite.
- **Pali, Prakrit, Tamil** — languages of the lower classes / commoners.
- Sanskrit texts were composed from a Brahminical perspective, so they can feel one-sided (not impartial).

## Key Source: The Mahabharata
- A colossal epic (*Mahakavya*) composed between **c. 500 BCE – 400 CE**, giving insight into early Indian society.
- Bharata and Puru tribes merged to form the **Kuru** lineage; Kuru later merged with Panchala to form **Kuru-Panchala**.

### The Critical Edition (1919–1966)
- Prepared under **V. S. Sukthankar**, starting in 1919 and completed in 1966 (47 years).
- Method: used the Sanskrit version as the base text and cross-checked it against regional versions (Bengali, Marathi, Odia, etc.).
- Ran to about **13,000 pages**.
- Finding: regional versions shared a common core but also had significant local variations — showing Sanskrit texts didn't transmit unchanged to commoners, likely due to social differences.
- This revealed two traditions:
  1. A dominant Brahminical tradition.
  2. A strong, resilient local tradition — regional communities did not strictly follow Sanskrit normative law books (Dharmashastras).

## Kinship Terms (Sanskrit)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kula | Kin |
| Jnati (not *Jaati*) | Kinfolk / relatives |
| Vansha | Lineage |

- **Patriliny** — descent traced father to son to grandson.
- **Matriliny** — descent traced through the mother.
- Hindu society largely followed patriliny; texts like the Mahabharata privilege the eldest son's right to inherit.
- **Kanyadan** (gift of a daughter) was considered one of a father's most important duties.
- Women (mothers and daughters) generally had no claim over family property.

## Marriage Rules and Practices
- **Endogamy** — marriage within one's own defined group (e.g., caste/varna) was the prescribed norm.
- **Exogamy** — marriage outside one's own kin group (gotra) was required; marrying within the same gotra (kin) was treated as incestuous and forbidden.
- **Polygyny** — a man having more than one wife.
- **Polyandry** — a woman having more than one husband; the Mahabharata references this through Draupadi. It was fairly common in earlier times before Brahmins began to disfavor it.

## The Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras
- **Dharmasutras**: composed c. 500–200 BCE.
- **Manusmriti**: compiled c. 200 BCE – 200 CE, later part of the wider Dharmashastra tradition.
- Context: with the rise of towns and cities, people began questioning Brahmanical authority, prompting Brahmins to codify a person's *dharma* (duty) in these texts.
- Dharmasutras classified marriage into two broad types:
  1. Approved forms (Brahma, Daiva, Prajapatya, etc.)
  2. Disapproved forms (Asura, Gandharva, etc.)

## Gotra of Women
- The gotra system was mainly followed in Brahmanical society.
- Rules:
  1. Marriage within the same gotra was prohibited.
  2. A woman adopted her husband's gotra after marriage.
- Gotras were named after Vedic seers (rishis).
- **Satavahana** queens retained their father's gotra even after marriage, and sons used their mother's name in their own — e.g., **Gautamiputra Satakarni** ("son of Gautami").
- This, along with references in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, shows the use of **metronymics** (name derived from the mother), as opposed to **patronymics** (name derived from the father).
- Note: using a mother's name (metronymic) is *not* the same as following a **matrilineal** system of descent — the Satavahanas did not practice matriliny.

## The Varna System
The Purushasukta hymn of the Rigveda lists four varnas:
1. Brahmana
2. Kshatriya
3. Vaishya
4. Shudra

As society diversified, this four-fold scheme could not account for every occupation, so **jati** (caste), tied to occupation, emerged alongside varna.

Examples of occupation-based jatis:
- Suvarnakara — goldsmith
- Lohakara — ironsmith
- Venukara — basket maker
- Rathakara — chariot maker

**General pattern:** caste ≈ occupational group, with "higher" castes associated with more prestigious occupations.

### Inter-Varna Marriage (Varna-sankara)
- **Anuloma vivaha** — higher-varna man marries lower-varna woman (more accepted).
- **Pratiloma vivaha** — higher-varna woman marries lower-varna man (condemned).
- Offspring of *pratiloma* unions were treated as outside the varna system (e.g., a Brahmana woman + Shudra man → considered a **Chandala**, an "untouchable").
- Over time, inter-caste marriage declined significantly.

### Caste Was Not Rigid in Practice
- People did change caste/occupation:
  - The **Mandsaur inscription** (5th century CE) records a guild of silk weavers from Lata (Gujarat) who migrated to Mandsaur and diversified into other occupations, including some becoming scholars.
  - In Shudraka's play ***Mricchakatikam***, the hero Charudatta is a Brahmana who works as a merchant.

## Buddhism, Jainism, and Critiques of Caste
- Buddhist texts (e.g., the Sutta Pitaka) describe a mythical golden age when people lived simply with no social distinctions.
- The term for the first king, elected by the people for his ideal qualities, is **Mahasammata** ("the great elect") — a Buddhist concept emphasizing that kingship, like caste, was a human/social construct, not divinely ordained.
- Buddhist texts include debates (e.g., in the Majjhima Nikaya) questioning birth-based hierarchy — for instance, asking whether a wealthy Shudra could command more respect/power than a poor Brahmana, challenging strict varna-based status.
- Buddhism, more broadly, argued that caste, kingship, and other social hierarchies are human-made and can be changed.

### Caste Norms vs. Reality
- Texts state only a Kshatriya could be king, yet outsider dynasties — **Shakas, Kushanas, Parthians, and Indo-Greeks (Yavanas)** — ruled as kings.
- Texts state only upper castes could use Sanskrit, yet the Shaka king **Rudradaman** issued a Sanskrit inscription at **Junagadh** (one of the earliest long Sanskrit inscriptions by a non-Brahmin, non-Kshatriya ruler).
- The **Satavahanas** claimed Brahmana status while also ruling as kings, and practiced endogamy — showing how caste categories were often blurred or reinterpreted to fit political reality.

## The Mahabharata as a Composite Text
1. It is both **didactic** (teaching moral/social duty) and **narrative** (telling a story).
2. It is believed that **charioteer-bards (sutas)** first narrated the story orally; Brahmins later composed it into written text around the **5th century BCE**, coinciding with the emergence of the Kuru-Panchala region as a political entity.
3. The text evolved over roughly 1,000 years:
   - **c. 200 BCE – 200 CE**: Krishna's role expanded, reflecting the growing popularity of Vishnu and his avatars.
   - **c. 200–400 CE**: didactic sections were added, coinciding with the compilation of the Manusmriti.
4. Archaeologist **B. B. Lal** excavated Hastinapur (Meerut, U.P.) around 1951–52 and identified five occupation levels:
   - **Period II** (c. 1100–800 BCE): unplanned, mud-brick, primitive houses (Painted Grey Ware).
   - **Period III** (c. 600–300 BCE): planned settlement with baked-brick structures, bathrooms, soakage jars, and ring wells — resembling a well-developed city of its time.
   - The Mahabharata war is traditionally dated to around **950 BCE**, so the epic's later descriptions of grand cities likely reflect later urban development retrospectively added into the narrative (or included simply for dramatic effect).
5. **Regional retellings**: The Mahabharata exists in many regional variations. In **Mahasweta Devi's** Bengali story *Kunti O Nishadi*, the plot is extended after the burning of the **Jatugriha** ("House of Lac") — imagining that Kunti secretly invited a Nishada woman and her five sons into the house before it was set on fire, and the Pandavas escaped.

| c. 300–600 CE | Other Dharmashastras (Sanskrit) |
| c. 400–500 CE | Kalidasa's plays; Aryabhata & Varahamihira's works on astronomy/mathematics (Sanskrit); Jaina works compiled (Prakrit) |
