# Kings, Farmers and Towns — Revised Notes

## 1. Harappan Timeline (for reference)
- Early phase: 6000–2600 BCE
- Mature phase: 2600–1900 BCE
- Later phase: 1900–1300 BCE
- Transition period linking to the next era: 1300–600 BCE

## 2. The Vedic Age and the "Aryan" Question
- The Rigveda's composition is generally dated to **c. 1500 BCE**.
- **R.E.M. (Mortimer) Wheeler**, historian and Director-General of the ASI, proposed the **Aryan Invasion Theory**, linking the decline of Harappan cities to an Aryan invasion.
- Rigvedic Aryans worshipped **Indra**, also called **"Purandara"** (fort-destroyer). Since the Rigveda is in Sanskrit (not the IVC's undeciphered script), Wheeler argued the Aryans were a distinct, possibly external, group.
- **Historians' current view:** "Aryan" refers to a **linguistic category** (speakers of Indo-European/Sanskrit-related languages), **not a race**. The racial interpretation was later exploited as Nazi propaganda and is now rejected by mainstream historians.
- Aryans were **nomadic pastoralists**, organised into **tribes (jana)**; a tribe's members shared kinship/blood ties. In Rigvedic usage, "Arya" broadly meant **"friends and kinsmen."**
- Tribes were likely organised as **clans (gotra)**, each with a **chief**, and tribal assemblies in which members (*jana*) participated formed a primitive tribal government.

### Vedic Period Timeline
- Overall: 1500–500 BCE
- Early Vedic phase: 1500–1000 BCE (Rigveda composed)
- Later Vedic phase: 1000–500 BCE (other three Vedas, Brahmanas, Upanishads composed)

## 3. Iron and Agrarian Expansion
- Iron came into widespread use by **c. 1000 BCE**.
- Iron tools let Aryan groups move into and clear the dense forests of the **Gangetic plains**, enabling a shift **from pastoralism to agriculture**.
- Conflicts shifted accordingly: earlier fought over **animals and grazing land**, later over **water and cultivable land**.

## 4. From Janapada to Mahajanapada
- Land occupied and settled by a *jana* became a territorial unit called a **janapada** ("where the foot/settlement of the jana stood"), usually named after the ruling clan.
- Chiefs gradually expanded their authority, collecting **grain as tax** to fund armies and courts — likely the origin of **hereditary kingship**.
- Chiefs commissioned law codes and sacred texts that portrayed the king as god-like, legitimising their rule.
- Smaller janapadas merged over time into **16 large Mahajanapadas** (c. 6th century BCE), mentioned in **Buddhist and Jain texts**.

## 5. Governance of the Mahajanapadas
1. **Monarchies** — e.g., Magadha, Avanti, Kosala, Vatsa
2. **Gana-sanghas (oligarchies)** — power shared among several rulers/chiefs collectively called *raja*; e.g., **Vajji and Malla**

- Mahajanapadas typically had **fortified capitals**, reflecting the need for defence and control of resources.
- As people resisted paying taxes, Brahmanas composed **Dharmasutras** declaring that only a **Kshatriya** could be king, that kings had the right to collect taxes and wage war, and that opposing one's own king was wrong.

### Key Janapadas and their Capitals
| Janapada | Capital |
|---|---|
| Magadha | Rajagriha/Girivraja → later Pataliputra |
| Avanti | Ujjaini |
| Kosala | Shravasti |
| Vatsa | Kaushambi |

## 6. Why Magadha Became Rich and Powerful (6th–4th century BCE)
1. Location near the **Ganga and its tributaries** aided farming and transport.
2. Rich **iron ore deposits** in the Bihar/Jharkhand region.
3. **Elephants** available from Magadhan forests, valuable for the army.
4. A succession of powerful, ambitious rulers and dynasties:
   - **Haryanka dynasty** (Bimbisara, Ajatashatru)
   - **Shishunaga dynasty**
   - **Nanda dynasty**
   - **Maurya dynasty**

## 7. The Mauryan Empire
- **Chandragupta Maurya** founded the empire, turning Magadha from a Mahajanapada into an empire.
- **Ashoka**, Chandragupta's grandson, took the empire to its peak.
- After the **Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE)**, Ashoka adopted and propagated **Dhamma**.

### Sources for Mauryan History
1. Greek writer **Megasthenes'** account, the *Indica*
2. **Alexander's invasion (326 BCE)**, which brought more Greeks into contact with the subcontinent
3. **Seleucus Nicator**, after his defeat by Chandragupta (c. 305 BCE), sent Megasthenes as an envoy to the Mauryan court
4. **Kautilya's Arthashastra**
5. Buddhist and Jain texts, the Puranas, and other Sanskrit works
6. **Ashokan inscriptions** (rock and pillar edicts) conveying messages of Dhamma

### Language and Script of Ashokan Inscriptions
- Most inscriptions: **Prakrit language in Brahmi script**, used across most of the subcontinent.
- In the **north-west**: Prakrit written in **Kharoshthi script**.
- In parts of **Afghanistan**: inscriptions in **Aramaic and Greek** languages/scripts, reflecting the region's population.
- The **Lion Capital of Sarnath** (India's State Emblem) — features an inverted lotus, an abacus, four lions, and a base with animal reliefs.

### Provincial Centres of the Mauryan Empire
| Centre | Significance |
|---|---|
| Takshashila (Taxila) | Centre of foreign (north-west) trade |
| Ujjaini | Centre of local/inland trade |
| Tosali | Near the sea coast (Odisha) |
| Suvarnagiri | Associated with gold |

### Megasthenes on Mauryan Administration
A military committee of **6 sub-committees, 5 members each**, managed: (1) the navy, (2) transport and provisions, (3) infantry, (4) cavalry, (5) chariots, and (6) elephants.

### Ashoka and Dhamma
Ashoka appointed special officers (**Dhamma Mahamattas**) to spread the message of Dhamma, helping him govern the vast empire.

### End of the Mauryan Empire
After roughly 150 years, the last Mauryan king, **Brihadratha**, was killed by his general **Pushyamitra Shunga** (c. 185 BCE), who founded the **Shunga dynasty**.

## 8. Decipherment of Ancient Scripts
- **James Prinsep**, an epigraphist, deciphered the **Brahmi** and **Kharoshthi** scripts in the 1830s.
- Brahmi is believed to be the ancestor of later scripts such as **Devanagari and Bengali**; Prinsep worked backward by comparing Brahmi letters with these later, related scripts.
- **Kharoshthi** was deciphered using **bilingual Indo-Greek coins**, which gave the same king's name in both Greek and Kharoshthi.
- Reading a Brahmi/Kharoshthi inscription, Prinsep found the title **"Priyadasi"** ("pleasant to behold"). Since a few other inscriptions used the name **"Ashoka"** alongside titles like Priyadasi and **Devanampiya**, historians concluded these titles referred to Ashoka.
- **Palaeography** (the study of how letter forms changed over time) helps historians date inscriptions.
- The earliest inscriptions were in **Prakrit**; **Tamil and Sanskrit** came into inscriptional use later.

## 9. Post-Mauryan Kingdoms (from ~2nd century BCE)
- **Mid India:** Satavahanas
- **North-East/North India:** Shunga dynasty
- **North-West (foreign dynasties):** Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushans
- The **Satavahanas, Kushans, and the southern Chera/Chola/Pandya chiefdoms** relied heavily on **long-distance trade** as a major source of revenue.
- The **Kushans** portrayed themselves as divine figures, practised ancestor worship, and commissioned large statues of themselves.

## 10. Southern Chiefdoms — the Sangam Age (c. 300 BCE–300 CE)
- Three major chiefly lineages of the far south: **Chola, Pandya, and Chera** (the Chera are referred to as "Keralaputra" in Ashokan inscriptions).
- Main source: **Sangam literature**, composed and compiled under the patronage of these chiefs — hence the period's name, the **Sangam Age**.
- These chiefs generally did **not collect regular taxes**; instead, they received **gifts and tribute** from people, including from long-distance traders.

## 11. The Gupta Empire and Political Structure (c. 320–550 CE)
Major contemporary powers: **Guptas** (from the 4th century CE), **Shakas**, **Vakatakas**, and the **Kalabhras** (South India).

- After the Mauryan decline, regional/subordinate rulers called **samantas** governed territories near larger empires.
- Under the Guptas, samantas offered homage, maintained armies for the emperor, and shared collected tax revenue with him.
- Gupta kings were not as singularly dominant as Ashoka, so they relied on **court poetry (prashasti kavya)** and grand titles to assert legitimacy.
  - The **Prayaga (Allahabad) Prashasti**, composed by **Harishena**, court poet of **Samudragupta**, portrayed him with the qualities of gods such as Indra, Varuna, Kubera, and Yama.
- The **Gandatindu Jataka** (a Pali story) describes a notorious king so unpopular that even animals in his kingdom resented him — cited as a cautionary tale about bad kingship.

## 12. Agrarian Developments
- **6th century BCE onward:** iron-tipped ploughshares made it possible to work the wet, muddy soil of the Ganga floodplain.
- **Paddy transplantation** significantly increased grain yields.
- Irrigation infrastructure — **ponds, wells, canals** — improved agricultural output.
- The **Sudarshana Lake** (Gujarat) was repaired by the Shaka ruler **Rudradaman** and later again by a Gupta ruler.
- Note: **Punjab and Rajasthan** continued to rely less on the plough (drier terrain, different cropping) even into more recent centuries — evidence of regional variation/inequality in agricultural development across ancient India.

### Gahapati and Land Grants
- **Gahapati** originally meant the male head of a household — owner of land, livestock, and with authority over dependents (including slaves and family). The term later broadened to mean "wealthy person."
- **Land grants**, formally recorded on copper plates or stone, began under the **Satavahanas**.
- **Prabhavati Gupta**, queen of the Vakatakas and daughter of **Chandragupta II**, is a well-documented example of a ruler who granted land — including administrative and taxation rights — to a recipient.
- Gupta-era land grants often came with exemptions from various obligations; kings extended grants beyond religious beneficiaries to officials as well.
- **Two main historical theories on land grants:**
  1. They were used to **bring new or underused land under cultivation**, extending the agrarian and revenue base of the state.
  2. They served **political purposes** — rewarding officials, pleasing restive samantas, or displaying royal generosity and piety.

## 13. Urbanization
- **First Urbanization:** the Indus Valley Civilization (Harappan cities).
- **Second Urbanization:** beginning in the **6th century BCE**, with cities such as **Puhar, Ujjaini, Pataliputra, and Mathura**.
  - **Mathura** functioned as a major commercial, political, and cultural centre.
- Sequence of major capitals: **Rajagriha → Pataliputra** (Magadha/Mauryan/Gupta) **→ Kannauj** (under Harshavardhana).
- **NBPW (Northern Black Polished Ware)**, a fine pottery, is found at wealthy urban sites alongside other elite/craft goods.

## 14. Trade, Guilds, and Coinage
- **Votive inscriptions** record gifts made to religious institutions, often by wealthy merchants and artisans, who organised themselves into **guilds (shrenis)**.
- Trade routes by the 6th century BCE:
  - **Overland**, via Central Asia, carried by caravan traders/peddlers.
  - **Maritime**, via the Arabian Sea (west coast) and the Bay of Bengal (to China and South-East Asia), carried in part by wealthy merchant-financiers (*shreshthis*).
- The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* records the **Malabar coast** exporting goods such as **pepper and malabathrum (a cinnamon-leaf product)**, alongside other traded goods like lead and copper.
- **Coinage:**
  - Coins came into use around the **6th century BCE**; the earliest were **punch-marked coins** (silver and copper), which did not name any king.
  - The **Indo-Greeks** were the first in the subcontinent to issue coins bearing the king's name and image.
  - The **Kushans** were the first to issue **gold coins** in significant quantity, influenced by Roman and Parthian coin designs.
  - Large numbers of **Roman coins** have been found in South India, reflecting Roman trade with the Chera, Chola, and Pandya kingdoms.
  - The **Yaudheyas**, a tribal republic contemporary with the Kushans/Guptas, issued their own copper coins.
  - **Gupta gold coins** are regarded as the purest/finest of ancient Indian gold coinage, though gold content in Gupta coinage declined from around the 6th century CE onward, likely reflecting economic and political pressures late in the dynasty.

## 15. Limitations of Inscriptional Evidence
- Letters, words, or entire inscriptions can be **damaged or illegible**.
- Not all inscriptions have been **discovered**; many likely remain lost or unfound.
- Content may be **incomplete, exaggerated, or biased**, reflecting the perspective and interests of whoever commissioned or wrote it.
